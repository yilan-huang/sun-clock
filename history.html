Ways the World Has Told Time

One fault line runs through the whole history of the clock: hours that stretch with the sun versus hours that stay fixed. For most of recorded history people used temporal (seasonal, unequal) hours tied to sunrise and sunset — the system this clock revives. The equal hour we live by now is the newcomer, standardised only because machines could not easily keep anything else.

I. The great divide

Every system is one of two kinds. Either the hour flexes with daylight, or it is a fixed slice of the whole day.

  • Temporal / seasonal / unequal hours: daylight is cut into a fixed count of hours, so each hour grows in summer and shrinks in winter.
  • Equal / equinoctial hours: the day is cut into a fixed length of hour, identical year-round — easy for gears, divorced from the sky.
  • Mechanical clocks are what tipped the world from the first to the second.

II. Ancient foundations

Egypt: the first 24

The 24-hour day is Egyptian. By the end of the third millennium BCE, priests divided the day using 36 star-groups called decans, 12 of which rose each night.1 From the late second millennium BCE, day and night were each reckoned as 12 hours — unequal, since daylight varies by season. Obelisks and shadow-clocks split the sunlit day; water clocks (the clepsydra) measured the night.2

Babylon: base-60

Our minutes and seconds are a Mesopotamian inheritance. Babylonian mathematics was sexagesimal (base-60); Hellenistic astronomers borrowed it to split the hour into 60.1 The day was also cut into 12 beru ("double-hours") of roughly two modern hours, and the night into watches. The same base-60 still rules clocks, compasses, and the 360° circle.

III. Hours that breathe with the sun

Greco-Roman temporal hours — what this clock runs on

Daylight was divided into 12 hours and night into 12, numbered from sunrise (0) to sunset (12), so an hour stretched and shrank with the season.3

  • At Mediterranean latitude a daytime hour ran ~45 min at midwinter and ~75 min at midsummer.
  • The sun peaked at the end of the 6th hour; hora sexta gave us siesta.
  • The 9th hour, nona, drifted from mid-afternoon to midday and became our word noon.4

Japanese Edo time

Japan kept temporal hours until 1872 and built clocks to match. Day and night were each split into six units (futeijihō), counted down 9→4 twice a day; the numbers 1–3 were reserved for Buddhist prayer strikes. Wadokei clocks used twin foliot balances to run at two speeds.5 The word for a snack, oyatsu, comes from yatsu ("eight") — the eighth-hour mid-afternoon break.

Prayer-time hours

Religious schedules preserved sun-anchored time long after astronomers moved on. The Christian canonical hours — prime (~6am), terce (~9am), sext (noon), none (~3pm), vespers — were the Roman temporal hours turned into prayer offices.6 Islamic ṣalāt ties five daily prayers to solar events; the Jewish proportional hour, sha'ah zmanit (day ÷ 12), is the same temporal maths, still used today.

Counting from an edge

Some systems kept equal-length hours but moved the zero point to a solar event — the practical cousin of this clock's "past sunrise / until sunset" readout. Babylonian hours counted up from sunrise; Italian (Bohemian) hours counted up from sunset. Ethiopia today uses a 12-hour clock counted from dawn, so ~6am = 0 and local 1:00 is our 7am — stable because the equator keeps days near-constant.7

IV. The cyclical day-counts of Asia

Chinese double-hours

China ran two systems at once: 12 shíchén (double-hours of ~2 hours), named for the zodiac Earthly Branches and starting at 11pm with the Rat; and the kè (刻) — 100 equal marks per day of ~14.4 minutes, a rare pre-modern decimal division.8 Night was split into five drum-and-bell watches (gēng).

Hindu / Vedic units

Indian timekeeping built a ladder from fractions of a second to the day, anchored at sunrise: 30 muhūrta of ~48 min each, running sunrise to sunrise.9 A ghaṭī (also nāḍikā / daṇḍa) ≈ 24 minutes, often kept by a sinking-bowl water clock; two ghaṭī = one muhūrta.

V. The decimal rebellions

French Revolutionary time

From 1793, revolutionary France decreed a 10-hour day, each hour 100 decimal minutes, each minute 100 decimal seconds — so noon fell at 5:00.10 Mandatory for only ~17 months: people would not re-learn the day. Metric length and mass survived; metric time did not.

Swatch Internet Time

A 1998 revival: the day split into 1000 ".beats" of 86.4 seconds (@000–@999), one global time with no zones, zeroed on Biel Mean Time at Swatch's Swiss HQ (UTC+1).11 Pitched for coordinating online play across borders; now a nostalgic curiosity.

VI. The modern machine

From sundial to mean time

The sun itself does not keep steady time. Apparent solar time (a sundial) speeds and slows across the year; averaging it gives mean solar time, and the gap is the Equation of Time (up to ~16 min). GMT is mean solar time at Greenwich; time zones freeze it into ~15°-wide bands; sidereal time tracks the stars (a sidereal day ≈ 23h 56m).

The atomic second & UTC

Since 1967 the second is defined by an atom, not the Earth: one SI second = 9,192,631,770 oscillations of the caesium-133 hyperfine transition.12 TAI is pure atomic time; UTC keeps it within 0.9s of the sun by inserting occasional leap seconds. This is the time on your phone and GPS — the baseline Horae converts back into sun-anchored hours.
